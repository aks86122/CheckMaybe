Title: `Gift cards: the expiry date and fee rules to check before you give one`
Subtitle: `Most gift card money can't expire for years. "Bonus" cards are a different story.`
```
A gift card feels like cash. Under federal rules, most of the time it mostly is. The exceptions are the cards people get handed at checkout this month.

The five-year rule. [verify] For a store or bank gift card, the money on it generally can't expire for at least five years from the date the card was bought or money was last loaded on it. If the plastic card expires sooner, you can ask for a replacement. [verify: whether the page says the replacement must be free]

Fees. [verify] A card can't charge an inactivity fee unless it hasn't been used for at least 12 months, and then no more than one fee a month. Any fees have to be disclosed on or with the card before you buy it.

The exception: bonus and promotional cards. "Buy a $50 gift card, get a $10 bonus card" is one of the most common holiday offers. [verify] Promotional cards like that bonus card aren't covered by the same rules, so they can expire much sooner, often within weeks. The expiry date should be on the card.

Here's what that looks like in a made-up example. You buy a $50 card in December and get a $10 bonus card that expires January 31. The $50 is still good in 2031. The $10 is gone in eight weeks if it sits in a drawer. Write the bonus card's date somewhere you'll see it.

Two more things worth knowing. Some states give you more than the federal rules, so check your state too. And if a store closes or goes bankrupt, a gift card can lose its value whatever the rules say, so don't sit on a big balance.

So when you give or get a gift card this month, check three things: is it a gift card or a bonus card, when does it expire, and are there any fees listed.

Made-up example. Educational, not legal or financial advice. Check the card's own terms.

Title: `A debt-free plan you can print (and the box that took a year)`
Subtitle: `Most printables give every box the same amount. This one comes from the plan, and the last box shows where it slows down.`
```
Most debt payoff printables give every box the same $100 and leave you to guess when you'll be done. I wanted one where every box is a real amount from the plan, and every milestone has a month next to it.

So I built the printable from the plan itself. In my made-up example, five debts add up to $19,150, with $1,500 a month going toward all of them. The page has 20 boxes for the total, one for every 5%, each with the month the plan gets there. Below that, each debt gets 10 boxes in the order you pay it off: the medical bill first because it's behind, the 0% store card next because its deal ends in May, then the 24.49% credit card.

The boxes fill fast at first. 50% by June 2027. 90% by November 2027.

Then the 95% box says May 2028, and the last one says December 2028. A whole extra year for the last 10%.

The reason is the personal loan, marked "Not sure" for early payoff. When you don't know whether a loan charges a fee for paying it off early, the plan doesn't put extra money on it. It pays the minimum and waits. So for a year, the plan sat on money it wasn't allowed to use.

In the example, if that loan allows early payoff with no fee, the debt-free month moves from December 2028 to December 2027, and the plan pays $189.32 less in interest. Same debts, same $1,500 a month. One answer from the lender.

That's the real use of a printable like this. Not the coloring, though that helps on the hard months. It's seeing where the plan slows down, and asking why.

So before you print yours, look at the last few boxes. If there's a long gap, find the debt that's waiting and the rule that's making it wait. Then make the call.
