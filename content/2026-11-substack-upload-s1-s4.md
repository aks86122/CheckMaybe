## s1｜排程：11/10（二） 22:00（台灣）

**Title**
```
Etsy is holding part of your money? What a payment reserve is and how to get it released
```
**Subtitle**
```
Three Etsy pages, read so you don't have to: why reserves happen, how long they last, and the fastest way out.
```
**內文**
```
A seller with five years on Etsy and a 99% on-time rate posted on r/EtsyCommunity that Etsy held half of every sale for three months over shipping delays. If you've just had the email, here's what Etsy's own pages say, in their words.

What a reserve is. From Etsy's Help Center: "a percentage of funds from each new sale of a physical item will be held and won't be available for deposit within the standard processing time." Not your whole balance, and not digital sales: "Sales of digital items are not held in reserve."

How big and how long. It varies, and the email tells you. The Etsy Payments Policy says that email "will include details of the Reserve, including the Reserve percentage applied to your Payment Account, the clearing window for individual payments, and the basis and timing on which your Reserve will be reviewed." The policy's own example is 30% for a 45-day rolling period, and it allows a variable reserve "for a period of up to 90 days." So keep that email. It's the closest thing you'll get to terms.

Why it happened. Etsy's 2023 Seller Handbook article names the two most common reasons: orders "consistently missing tracking information or tracking events, especially for orders over $100" and "Orders aren't shipped on time." Other triggers it lists include a sudden jump in orders or refunds, a first sale, "a sale after a long period of inactivity", and a history of low reviews.

Your shop isn't hidden. Same article: "Even if a reserve is placed on your account, your shop and listings are still active and visible to shoppers and you can continue to make sales." If your traffic dropped at the same time, the reserve isn't what Etsy says caused it.

The fastest way out, per order. Add tracking. Funds are released when "Etsy can verify the tracking shows the order is in transit." The catch: "Etsy isn't always able to confirm tracking for some carriers," and then that order waits out the full period. You can see each order's release date in your monthly statement CSV, under "Availability Date".

Getting the reserve itself lifted. I didn't find an appeal form for reserves in any of the three pages. What Etsy says is that it "regularly evaluate[s] accounts with a Payment account reserve to determine whether the reserve is still needed", and that a reserve can be reduced or removed after "a reduction in cases or an improvement in the seller's fulfillment of orders." So the practical route is to fix whatever the email names, then contact Etsy Support and ask for a review, with your tracking and on-time numbers ready. And one line worth knowing before you plan around it: "If you're already a Star Seller, a reserve will not be placed on your shop."

Rules change, so check Etsy's current pages yourself. This is educational, not financial or legal advice, and I'm not affiliated with Etsy.

## s2｜排程：11/12（四） 22:00（台灣）

**Title**
```
Black Friday on pay-in-4? Add up January before you check out
```
**Subtitle**
```
Each plan looks small on its own. The bill that matters is the month they all land.
```
**內文**
```
Pay-in-4 at checkout feels like nothing: four small payments, often no interest. The problem usually isn't one plan. It's four of them, started the same week, all landing in the same January.

Here's a made-up example from the sheet I use. Four holiday purchases go on payment plans: two short pay-in-4 plans and two monthly ones. December's payments come to $498.00. January's come to $428.25, which is 10.2% of the example income, before rent, groceries or the credit card bill. February through April are still $148.50 each. None of those numbers shows up on the checkout page. You only see them if you add the plans up yourself.

Not every plan is 0%, either. In the same example, a $499 console paid as 6 × $89.50 comes to $537. That works out to about 25.7% APR. A $649 TV as 12 × $59 comes to $708, about 16.4% APR. The monthly price looked fine in both cases. The total didn't.

So before you click "pay later" this week, run five quick checks. What will I pay in total? Roughly what yearly rate is that? What happens if I'm late, or don't finish on time? When does the first payment leave? And what else is due that month?

The fourth one catches people more than you'd think. Some plans take the first payment at checkout, some two weeks later, and they don't line up with payday.

The last one is the reason this post exists. Write every plan you start this week on one list, with its dates, and look at January as a single number.

## s3｜排程：11/17（二） 22:00（台灣）

**Title**
```
Is that Black Friday deal real? Three numbers to check first
```
**Subtitle**
```
The usual price, the price at checkout, and the price on a payment plan.
```
**內文**
```
"Black Friday deal" covers a lot of prices that aren't deals. Three numbers tell you which is which, and you can check all three in a couple of minutes.

The first is the usual price. Compare the sale price with what the item normally sells for, not with the crossed-out price on the banner. In my made-up example list, headphones at $199 that usually sell for $249 are 20.1% below the usual price. That's a real deal. An air fryer at $89 that usually sells for $89 is not a deal at all, whatever the banner says.

The second is the checkout price. Tax and shipping get added after you've decided. A $159 robot vacuum with 8% tax and $15 shipping is $186.72 at checkout. Still maybe a good price, but it's the number to compare.

The third is the price on a payment plan. A sale on a monthly plan can cost more than paying in full at the usual price. In the example, a $499 TV is $538.92 at checkout. On 12 payments of $49 it comes to $588. That's $49.08 more than paying in full, which eats most of the $50 discount.

If the item isn't something you needed before the sale started, that's a fourth check, and it's the one that saves the most.

## s4｜排程：11/19（四） 22:00（台灣）

**Title**
```
Snowball or avalanche? Check your 0% end dates first
```
**Subtitle**
```
The cheapest payoff order on paper can cost more when a promo deal ends.
```
**內文**
```
Snowball says pay the smallest balance first. Avalanche says pay the highest interest rate first. Both assume every debt behaves the same way until it's paid. Real contracts don't.

The one that changes the answer most often is a 0% deal with deferred interest. Some store cards and financing offers work like this: if any of the promotional balance is still unpaid when the promotion ends, interest can be charged going back to the day you bought it, not just on what's left. The US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau has warned that these charges catch people by surprise.

Here's what that does to a plan. In my made-up example, five debts, $1,500 a month toward all of them. On paper, avalanche pays the least interest: $1,598.68, against $1,679.15 for the order I'd actually use. But avalanche leaves the 0% store card for later, because its rate is zero, and clears it after the promo ends in May. If that card has deferred interest, back-interest lands on top, and "cheapest" stops being true. The other order pays the store card off in time.

Two more rules can flip the order. Some loans charge a fee for paying them off early, so extra money is better spent elsewhere until the fee ends. And some debts you're behind on should come first, whatever their rate, because that's where the late fees and collection calls are.

So before you pick snowball or avalanche, write down three things for each debt: when any 0% deal ends, whether it has deferred interest, and whether paying it early costs a fee. Then compare.
